Claude Code extensions · comparison · 3 August 2026

One writes the rules down. The other wires them into the harness.

superpowers ships fourteen skill documents and a single hook that tells the model those documents exist. claudetools ships one skill document and ten hook scripts across six lifecycle events. Almost every overlap and every gap between the two projects falls out of that one structural difference.

Both, however, are optimizing the same thing — token spend across delegated work — and they reached the same invariant independently. Where they disagree is worth more than where they overlap: delegation economics.

Deep dive · delegation economics

Both are optimizing token spend. They disagree about how.

The easy reading is that superpowers delegates for focus and claudetools delegates for cost. That reading is wrong. superpowers ships a four-tier model policy with an explicit cost thesis, and its design notes carry per-run dollar figures. The two projects independently arrived at the same invariant, in almost the same words.

“Use the least powerful model that can handle each role to conserve cost and increase speed.”

superpowers · subagent-driven-development/SKILL.md — shipped

“Keep for yourself: ALL reasoning — design decisions, correctness/security judgment, tradeoffs… For a task that needs reasoning, SPLIT it: have the runner gather the raw material and return a compact report, then you reason over the report.”

claudetools · task-gopher directive — shipped

That is convergent evolution, not overlap to be resolved. Two projects reaching the same rule from different directions is evidence the rule is correct. superpowers' internal design notes put the same guardrail more sharply still — “cheapen mechanics, never judgment,” with the requirement that every cheapened decision be shown mechanical: deterministic, scriptable, or cheaply verifiable after the fact. The disagreements are all downstream of the shared rule.

Divergence 1 — where the model decision lives

the sharpest difference

superpowers · per dispatch

The controller picks a tier every time, from a ladder: mechanical 1–2 file tasks → cheap; multi-file integration → standard; architecture and the final whole-branch review → most capable; reviews scaled to the diff's risk; a stuck fix-loop escalates “at least one tier above the implementer that got stuck.”

claudetools · per role

The model is assigned once, to a role, and lives in the agent definition — /hierarchy set reviewer opus, task-gopher hard-pinned to Haiku. The dispatching agent does not choose and cannot forget.

Why it matters

superpowers documents the failure mode itself: an omitted model: “inherits your session's model — often the most capable and most expensive — which silently defeats this section.” A policy written in prose has to be remembered at every dispatch. A policy written in frontmatter does not.

Divergence 2 — what the cheapest tier is actually for

less a divergence than it first reads

superpowers · stated as a judgment

“Turn count beats token price … the cheapest models routinely take 2–3× the turns on multi-step work — costing more overall. Use a mid-tier model as the floor for reviewers and for implementers working from prose descriptions. When the task's plan text contains the complete code to write, the implementation is transcription plus testing: use the cheapest tier.”

claudetools · stated as a contract

task-gopher pins the runner to Haiku and constrains what may be sent to it: fully specified, decision-free orders only. The runner never reasons, fills gaps, or chooses — it stops and reports back rather than guessing.

The difference

The governing variable is not model tier — it is specification completeness. The 2–3× multiplier is measured on cheap models asked to derive what to do from prose; where the work is fully specified, superpowers reaches for the cheapest tier itself. The Haiku pin is not a challenge to that finding, it operates inside its carve-out by construction — the same rule, once as a judgment the controller makes per dispatch, once as a contract the runner enforces.

Where the churn relocates

the honest caveat

the soft joint

“Stop if the order is ambiguous” asks the runner to recognize ambiguity — a judgment call handed to the model instructed not to judge. It can fail in both directions: proceed on an ambiguous order, or bounce on a clear one.

where the cost lands

A bounce costs an orchestrator turn plus a re-dispatch — and the orchestrator is the largest line item in the breakdown below. A high bounce rate does not remove churn; it moves it onto the most expensive model in the system.

And the residual risk is quality, not turns

A runner that does not reason also does not flag an implausible result. Constraining it to mechanical work does not eliminate reasoning failures — it relocates the requirement to the orchestrator's review of the report.

DIVERGENCE 3 — WHAT GETS MEASURED

superpowers' design spec breaks one plan-execution run down by component. The shape of that breakdown is the most useful thing in either repo for anyone tuning delegation:

Controller — session model, opus $6–7
Implementers — sonnet, 10–13 dispatches $5–6
Task reviewers — sonnet, 10 $1–1.5
Final review + fixes $1

one run, measured config≈ $13

The controller is the single largest line item — driven by “~150 turns × resident context” with a “prompt-immune turn floor (46% thinking/narration).” Not the workers. The workers are the cheap part.

WHAT THIS IMPLIES FOR CLAUDETOOLS

The measurement validates output-discipline more than it validates the Haiku pin. If the coordinator's resident context is half the bill, then the highest-value lever is whatever keeps tool output out of the orchestrator — the PreToolUse Bash gate and task-gopher's “dispatch must COMPRESS” rule — not which model runs the grep.

“Prompt-immune turn floor” is a warning about directive stacking. They measured 46% of controller turns going to thinking and narration and found it did not respond to prompt changes. claudetools injects four directives at SessionStart; that is a cost hypothesis worth testing rather than assuming.

The methodology is the borrowable part. Blind A/B with a planted-defect quality gate over N=5 runs is a real answer to “did this actually save money, or move it.” /hierarchy usage already collects the spend side at zero model cost; pairing it with a quality gate is the next step, and superpowers has demonstrated the shape.

The number that settles the cheap-tier question is bounce rate — the fraction of dispatches returning incomplete, blocked, or needing takeover. Near zero, and pinning the runner beats the mid-tier floor for this scope. High, and the churn has simply moved onto the orchestrator. It is cheap to instrument: SubagentStop already parses transcripts for token counts, and classifying a report as complete-versus-bounced is a text check, not a model call.

Genuine overlap — three more cases

The same problem, approached on a different axis

Separating the plan from the build

superpowers

writing-plans produces a document of self-contained 2–5 minute tasks; executing-plans loads it in a fresh session, reviews it critically, and works through it with verification checkpoints.

claudetools

The Architect writes a spec file at a dictated path and cannot run anything — no Edit, no Bash. The Implementor reads that spec, and when the spec is silent or wrong it stops and reports upward rather than choosing.

The difference

superpowers separates plan from build in time — two sessions, one artifact between them. claudetools separates them by capability: the planner is denied the tools that would let it start building.

Code review as a dispatched role

superpowers

requesting-code-review dispatches a reviewer at milestones with hand-crafted context. receiving-code-review covers the other half — verify the feedback is technically correct instead of performing agreement.

claudetools

The hierarchy's Reviewer is read-only by construction and never executes — it delegates every test run to the runner. Each finding is classified impl-defect or spec-defect, which routes it back to the Implementor or the Architect.

The difference

superpowers is the only one of the two with anything to say about receiving a review. claudetools is the only one that turns a verdict into a routing decision.

Surviving compaction via SessionStart

superpowers

Injects the using-superpowers skill body wrapped in <EXTREMELY_IMPORTANT>, declaring that skills exist and must be invoked before any response — including before asking a clarifying question.

claudetools

All four plugins inject at SessionStart, and each injects the rule text itself rather than a pointer to it — the delegation directive, the comment rule, the command-output rules.

The difference

Identical mechanism, opposite payload. superpowers injects a table of contents and spends its budget on discovery; claudetools injects the rules and spends its budget on compliance.

No counterpart on the other side

What each one has alone

ONLY IN CLAUDETOOLS

  • Hard blocking A PreToolUse gate denies tail -f, watch, and unfiltered test runs before they execute. superpowers registers no PreToolUse hook at all, so nothing it asks for is enforceable.
  • Rewriting a dispatch in flight task-gopher edits the Agent tool's prompt via updatedInput so every subagent carries the directive. An undocumented harness capability with no analogue in superpowers.
  • Policy that reaches subagents comment-discipline uses SubagentStart; task-gopher uses the Agent rewrite. superpowers deliberately hands each subagent hand-crafted context and never the session's history — a sound choice for task context, but it leaves standing policy with no channel at all.
  • Per-role model assignment /hierarchy pins a model to each of the six roles, and SubagentStop records usage per role at zero token cost.
  • Tool denial as design Reasoning roles are denied execution; the Reviewer is denied writes. superpowers ships no agent definitions, so it has no place to express this.
  • Model choice that cannot be omitted The model lives in the agent definition, not in the dispatch prompt. superpowers warns that an omitted model: "silently defeats" its own cost section — a failure mode that is structurally impossible here.
  • Context-flood defense Capture to a file, grep it, report the exit code; background long processes and wait on a line. An entire discipline with no counterpart.
  • Comment hygiene Re-injected after every Edit/Write, because the rule decays exactly when the model is mid-diff.

ONLY IN SUPERPOWERS

  • Test-driven development Red–green–refactor as a mandated cycle: the failing test comes before the implementation. claudetools has no opinion on testing whatsoever.
  • A brainstorming gate Required before any creative work — a nine-step pass over intent and requirements before code is touched.
  • Systematic debugging Four phases — investigation, pattern analysis, hypothesis testing, implementation — before any fix is proposed.
  • Verification before completion Run the command fresh and read the output before claiming anything passes. Evidence before assertion.
  • Workspace isolation and landing Worktree detection and setup up front; then merge, PR, or keep-as-is with cleanup once the tests pass.
  • A meta-skill for authoring skills writing-skills teaches test-driven documentation: baseline the agent's behavior first, then write the reference, then verify compliance changed.
  • Portability Manifests for Codex, Cursor, Kimi, Gemini, and opencode. Documents travel between hosts; hooks do not.
  • Measured cost, not asserted cost A design spec carrying per-run dollar figures, a component-level cost breakdown, and quality gates over N=5 runs with blind A/B parity. claudetools collects per-role spend at zero model cost; the matching quality gate is open work.
  • An ecosystem around it A sibling marketplace with ten plugins — Chrome DevTools access, episodic memory over past sessions, a private journal MCP, a session driver.

Running both

Where they compose, and where they fight

COMPOSES WELL

  • verification-before-completion × output-discipline superpowers demands you run the command and read the output. That is precisely the flood output-discipline gates. Together they produce the right shape: run it, capture it, grep it, report the exit code.
  • subagent-driven-development × the task-gopher relay superpowers is right that a subagent should not inherit your session history — but standing policy is not history. The PreToolUse Agent rewrite carries rules to every child without carrying the transcript.
  • Skills as the what, hooks as the who Fourteen documents describing a process, plus a control plane deciding which model runs each step and what it may touch. Neither repo covers the other's half.
  • TDD × the hierarchy The Implementor is the natural home for red–green–refactor, and the Reviewer's spec-defect classification is a cleaner destination for a failing test than a generic review comment.

FRICTION TO EXPECT

  • Contention for the top of the context superpowers wraps its injection in <EXTREMELY_IMPORTANT> and demands a skill invocation before any response. task-gopher demands a dispatch first. Two "before you do anything" mandates arriving in the same block.
  • Two org charts for one question writing-plans/executing-plans and Architect→Implementor are competing answers to the same problem. Running both is redundancy, not depth — pick one and let the other stay uninstalled.
  • Asymmetric survival on resume superpowers does not match resume. On a resumed session your directives are present and its mandate is not, so behavior silently diverges from a fresh session.
  • Skills that ask for denied tools dispatching-parallel-agents assumes the caller can dispatch. Inside an agent-hierarchy reasoning role, that tool may be denied — the skill will ask for something the role cannot do.

Design rationale

Why gates, and where they stop working

The failure this repo exists to prevent is not disobedience. No model reads “delegate the legwork” and decides to defy it. What happens is erosion through locally-reasonable exceptions: this grep is tiny, that file is half in context already, dispatching has overhead, the answer is needed now. Every individual call is defensible. The aggregate is the directive ignored.

Prose defends against this badly, because the rationalization feels like judgment from the inside — and judgment is exactly what the model has been told to keep for itself. So the useful question is not “is this rule written down” but how many times must the model choose to obey it. That gives a ladder.

1 Structurally impossible The decision does not exist to be eroded — model pinned in agent frontmatter, Reviewer denied Write, Architect denied Bash.
2 Hard-denied Blocked at PreToolUse — output-discipline's Bash gate. Only safe for machine-checkable invariants: tail -f is wrong every time.
3 Checkpoint, with an escape hatch Interrupted, must be justified, may proceed — task-gopher's strict mode. For rules where the call genuinely needs judgment and a deny would sometimes be wrong.
4 Injected directive Restated at SessionStart, SubagentStart, PostToolUse — survives compaction, but is still re-decided every turn.
5 Prose in a document Read once, decays with distance and context pressure. A CLAUDE.md paragraph, a SKILL.md section.

The principle underneath

A directive is a decision the model must re-make every turn. A structure is a decision made once.

Read that way, the strongest work in this repo is not the blocking — it is rung 1. A model pinned in an agent definition is not enforced, it is unforgettable: there is no per-dispatch choice left to erode. The gates are the enforcement arm of a larger principle, which is to move policy out of the space of per-turn decisions entirely, and to gate only what has to stay behavioral. Gate mechanics; nudge judgment.

The strongest independent evidence for this comes from the other side of this comparison. superpowers closes its Model Selection section with a confession about its own medium:

“Always specify the model explicitly when dispatching a subagent. An omitted model inherits your session's model — often the most capable and most expensive — which silently defeats this section.”

superpowers · subagent-driven-development/SKILL.md

That is a document observing that documents fail, and prescribing more document. Their own measurement found the ceiling directly: a “prompt-immune turn floor” of 46% of controller turns spent on thinking and narration that did not respond to prompt changes. They located the limit of prompt-only control empirically and kept building beneath it.

WHERE THIS ARGUMENT STOPS

  • Judgment cannot be gated. “Is this step reasoning or legwork?” is not machine-checkable, which is why task-gopher ships a checkpoint rather than a deny. The thing you most want — good delegation decisions — lives on the nudge side of the line and always will. Hooks compress the rationalization space; they cannot close it.
  • Every gate is a tax on every turn. False positives cost real time — this repo already documents one. And each injected directive is resident context on every request, which is the exact line item superpowers measured as the largest cost in their system. Four directives is fine; the drift toward ten is where a control plane starts costing what it saves.
  • Compliance is demonstrated; efficacy is not. That the gates fire and change behavior is observable. Whether gated delegation nets out cheaper than good prompts plus nothing is measured nowhere — by either project. Applying the same standard to these plugins that they apply to the model is the open work.

The short version

They are not competitors

Both projects talk about subagents, plans, and review, but superpowers describes those as processes a model should choose to follow and claudetools implements them as constraints a model cannot route around. superpowers would gain enforcement from these hooks; claudetools would gain a methodology it has no opinion about, and a measurement discipline it has not yet applied to itself.

Two pieces are genuinely duplicated, and each is a decision rather than a merge: the plan-then-build split (writing-plans/executing-plans versus Architect→Implementor), and the model-tier policy — where the same rule is written twice, once as prose the controller reads at dispatch time and once as a value fixed in an agent definition.
